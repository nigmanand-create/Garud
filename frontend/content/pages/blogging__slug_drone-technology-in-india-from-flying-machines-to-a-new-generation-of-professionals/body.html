Drone Technology in India- From Flying Machines to a New Generation of Professionals

DroneHub · Oct 2, 2026 · 8 min read · 4 views

Drone Technology in India: From Flying Machines to a New Generation of Professionals By Dr. Manoj Bhardwaj Ph.D. in Mechanical Engineering | Remote Pilot Instructor (DGCA) | Mechanical Engineering Faculty | 18+ Years of Teaching Experience The Drone Revolution Is Already Here Technology has always changed the way we work, but some technologies change much more than our tools—they change the way we think about an entire industry. Drone technology is one such development. During my more than 18 years of teaching Mechanical Engineering, I have seen students become familiar with many emerging technologies. However, the growth of drones in India has been particularly interesting because drones bring together almost every aspect of modern engineering—mechanical design, aerodynamics, electrical systems, electronics, sensors, communication, software, control systems and field operations. A decade ago, when we talked about drones, many people thought of them mainly as flying cameras or equipment used for photography. Today, that perception has changed considerably. Drones are finding applications in agriculture, land surveying, mapping, infrastructure inspection, construction, mining, disaster management, cinematography, environmental monitoring and several other areas. More importantly, drones are creating a completely new professional ecosystem. And this is where I see a significant opportunity for India's young engineers, diploma holders, ITI students and technical professionals. A Drone Is Much More Than a Flying Machine From a distance, a multirotor drone may look like a relatively simple machine with motors and propellers. But once we start understanding how it actually works, we realise how much engineering is involved. A drone has a structural frame, propulsion system, motors, propellers, electronic speed controllers, battery, flight controller, sensors, communication systems and, in many cases, specialised payloads. Every component has a role. The frame has to be sufficiently strong without adding unnecessary weight. The motors and propellers must generate adequate thrust. The battery must provide the required energy while maintaining an acceptable weight. Sensors have to provide accurate information to the flight controller. The complete system must work together in a controlled and reliable manner. For a Mechanical Engineer, this opens up an interesting field. Questions such as How do we reduce weight? How do we control vibration? Which material is suitable? How much thrust is required? How can the propulsion system become more efficient? How can payload capacity be increased? These are not simply drone questions. These are engineering questions. That is why I believe Mechanical Engineering students have a natural opportunity to contribute to the growing drone industry. India Needs More Than Drone Pilots One of the things I have observed while interacting with students and professionals is that many people associate the drone industry only with becoming a drone pilot. Certainly, trained and responsible remote pilots are an important part of the industry. But the complete drone ecosystem is much larger. We need people who can assemble, inspect, maintain, troubleshoot and understand the technology behind the drone. We need technicians. We need engineers. We need instructors. We need application specialists. We need people who can work with drone data. We need entrepreneurs who can identify practical problems and develop drone-based solutions. We also need professionals who understand aviation safety and regulatory requirements. Therefore, when we talk about careers in the drone sector, we should not restrict the discussion to flying alone. The future will require a combination of technical knowledge, practical skills and professional discipline. The Importance of Practical Training As a teacher, I have always believed that engineering cannot be completely understood through books and classrooms alone. A student may understand the theory of thrust, lift, torque, battery capacity or motor performance. But when that student actually holds a drone, examines its components, checks the propulsion system or tries to identify a fault, the learning becomes different. It becomes practical. This is particularly important in drone education. Students should not only learn how to fly a drone. They should also understand:  How the drone is designed and assembled  How the propulsion system works  How batteries should be handled  How pre-flight inspections are carried out  How basic faults can be identified  How different payloads are integrated  How weather and operating conditions affect performance  Why operational procedures and safety are important The classroom gives us the foundation. Practical exposure gives that foundation meaning. The Connection Between Drones and Mechanical Engineering My own academic background in Mechanical Engineering naturally makes me look at drone technology from an engineering perspective. A drone is an excellent example of multidisciplinary engineering. The structural design involves strength, stiffness, material selection and weight optimisation. The propulsion system involves motors, propellers, thrust, torque and efficiency. Vibration becomes important because excessive vibration can affect both mechanical components and sensitive electronic systems. Thermal management becomes relevant in motors, batteries and electronic components. Aerodynamics becomes important when we consider efficiency, payload and flight performance. As drones become larger and more sophisticated, these engineering considerations become even more important. This is why I encourage Mechanical Engineering students not to think that drone technology belongs only to electronics or computer science. There is a significant place for Mechanical Engineering in the drone industry. From Classroom Learning to Industry Skills India has no shortage of technically educated young people. The real challenge is to convert education into industry-ready skills. This is where educational institutions and industry need to work together. A student completing a technical course should ideally have an opportunity to see the equipment, work with the system and understand how the technology is used in the field. For drone technology, this could mean exposure to: Drone assembly and maintenance Simulator-based training Practical flying Battery and propulsion systems Pre-flight and post-flight procedures Drone applications Basic troubleshooting Safety and regulatory awareness Such exposure can help students understand whether they want to build a career as a remote pilot, drone technician, engineer, instructor, application specialist or entrepreneur. The Opportunity for India's Youth India is at an interesting stage in the development of its drone ecosystem. For young people, this is a good time to start learning about the technology—not simply because drones are popular today, but because their applications are continuing to expand. A student with a Mechanical Engineering background can explore drone structures and propulsion. An Electronics student can work on sensors, controllers and communication systems. A Computer Science student can explore automation, image processing, artificial intelligence and data analytics. A student interested in field operations can develop professional remote piloting skills. Someone with an entrepreneurial mindset can look at agriculture, surveying, inspection, logistics and other sectors where drones can solve practical problems. The opportunities are therefore not limited to one academic stream. What I Tell My Students Whenever I discuss emerging technologies with students, I tell them one thing: Do not follow technology only because it is fashionable. Understand the technology. The drone industry will continue to evolve. Today's equipment will not necessarily be the same tomorrow. What will remain valuable is the ability to understand a system, solve a problem, work safely and continuously learn. A certificate may open a door, but knowledge and practical competence help a professional stay relevant. For students entering the drone sector, I would therefore recommend building three things together: Knowledge + Practical Skills + Professional Discipline This combination can make a much stronger foundation for a long-term career. Looking Ahead The future of drones in India is not only about better cameras, longer flight times or higher payloads. The larger story is about applications and solutions. How can a farmer use a drone more efficiently? How can infrastructure be inspected faster and more safely? How can surveying become more efficient? How can difficult or dangerous locations be monitored? How can drone data help organisations make better decisions? These are the questions that will shape the next stage of the industry. And behind these applications, there will be a growing requirement for professionals who understand both the technology and its practical use. Conclusion Drone technology is no longer simply a fascinating flying machine. It is becoming an important part of India's emerging technology and engineering ecosystem. From my experience as a Mechanical Engineering faculty member and Remote Pilot Instructor (DGCA), I believe that our focus should now move beyond simply teaching people how to fly. We should develop professionals who understand the machine, the technology, the application and the responsibility that comes with operating an unmanned aircraft system. For India's young generation, drones can offer an exciting combination of engineering, technology, entrepreneurship and career opportunities. The machines may be getting smarter every year, but one thing will remain unchanged: The success of the drone industry will ultimately depend on the knowledge, skills and professionalism of the people behind the technology. And that is where education has an important role to play. About the Author Dr. Manoj Bhardwaj is a Mechanical Engineering professional and educator with 18+ years of teaching experience. He holds a Ph.D. in Mechanical Engineering and has professional experience in the DGCA-regulated drone sector as a Remote Pilot Instructor (DGCA). His professional interests include drone technology, Mechanical Engineering, electric and hybrid vehicles, technical education and skill development.
